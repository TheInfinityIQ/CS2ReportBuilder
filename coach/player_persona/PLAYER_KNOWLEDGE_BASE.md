# CS2 player knowledge base

Version 0.1 — 2026-10-07. Background for an experienced, non-professional
competitive player persona. This is a portable working draft, not a validated
representation of the average CS2 player.

This file describes available understanding. It assigns no rank, hours played,
personal match history, preferred role or execution weaknesses. Knowledge of a
concept and consistent execution of it are different things.

## 1. The game as a changing round state

CS2 decisions combine mechanics, information, positioning, timing, utility,
economy and team coordination. Aim matters, but does not explain every result.
The value of an action depends on what it achieves for the round.

Relevant state includes the score, remaining time, alive players, health,
weapons, remaining utility, bomb location, map control, known enemy locations,
teammate positions and routes that have or have not been cleared. A decision can
be reasonable with the information available and still fail. A poor decision can
succeed because the opponent misses or makes a mistake.

Kills can create impact, but impact also comes from gaining space, enabling
trades, delaying a hit, supplying information and preserving equipment. Low kills
alone do not establish poor play; high kills alone do not establish good decisions.

## 2. Information, uncertainty and opponent reads

Confirmed sightings and sounds differ from guesses based on timing, utility or
previous rounds. Information becomes less reliable as time passes and opponents
can move. One player seen at a site does not establish that the whole team is there.

Utility can suggest pressure or intent without proving a commit. A missing
opponent can be holding, rotating, lurking or somewhere not yet checked. Last
known position and current position are different claims.

Game sense combines available information, geometry, travel time and plausible
opponent choices. A repeated pattern can support a read, but one occurrence is
weak evidence. Conditioning means showing a pattern that opponents may learn;
masking means making different plans look similar from their perspective.
Neither guarantees how they will respond.

## 3. Time, space and exposure

Timing includes arrival windows, coordinated actions and moments when an opponent
is occupied, repositioning or unable to support a teammate. Spawn, route,
movement, noise and utility can change these windows.

Map control creates options and limits enemy movement. Occupying an area is more
useful when teammates can maintain it, receive information from it or exploit it.
Space can become unsafe again after a defender leaves or enough time passes.

Pressure is a credible threat that draws attention, utility or rotations.
Creating pressure and getting a kill are related but different outcomes. Giving
up all control can make a numerical advantage harder to convert.

Good pathing limits simultaneous exposure, uses cover and supports manageable
crosshair transitions. The shortest route is not always the safest or most useful.

## 4. Aim and movement

Crosshair placement reduces the correction needed when an enemy appears. It
depends on likely position, elevation and expected peek width. Pre-aim means
setting up for a threat before exposing to it; a flick is a rapid correction
afterward. Strong setup can reduce dependence on large flicks.

Movement state affects weapon accuracy. Counter-strafing is a way to stop lateral
movement quickly; the practical issue is whether the weapon is sufficiently
accurate when the shot is fired. Weapon, stance, range, recoil and recovery matter.

Tapping, bursting and spraying suit different ranges and situations. Recoil
control and spray transfers are familiar concepts. A missed duel may involve
movement, exposure or crosshair setup as well as mouse control. A death does not
by itself prove which mechanic failed.

## 5. Peeking and positioning

A dry peek has no supporting utility. A wide swing commits farther into the
angle. A shoulder or jiggle peek can seek information or bait a shot with less
commitment, but predictable exposure can still be punished.

Re-peeking can make sense with a concrete advantage such as support, a displaced
opponent or a recovery window. Repeating the same exposed duel without a change
can give the opponent an easier fight.

An off-angle avoids common pre-aim but may sacrifice cover or escape. A
one-and-done position has limited prospects after its surprise is revealed.
Angle isolation limits how many enemies can fight at once. Whether a position
is good depends on information, support, utility, timing and the objective.

## 6. Trading and teammate geometry

A trade or refrag kills an opponent shortly after they kill a teammate, before
they can freely escape or reset. Distance alone does not establish tradeability:
sightlines, obstacles, facing, timing and the follower's readiness also matter.

Useful spacing lets a teammate convert contact while reducing the chance that
one spray, flash or grenade disables both players. It is not a universal distance.
An entry can create value by revealing positions or opening space even when
they die, provided the team can convert it.

A synchronized double peek complicates the defender's fight. A crossfire creates
complementary threats; two players near each other do not automatically form one.
Staying behind can be selfish baiting or a coordinated setup. The distinction
requires purpose and context rather than scoreboard order alone.

## 7. Utility as a tool

A smoke blocks sightlines and can enable a crossing, isolate defenders, delay
pressure, conceal information or support planting and retaking. Blocking a lane
also affects teammates' information and movement options.

A flash creates a temporary opportunity. Its value depends on who can act during
that opportunity, their position and whether enemies can avoid it. A pop flash
offers little warning after becoming visible. Self-flashes and support flashes
serve different users; anti-flash positioning prepares for an expected flash.

Fire can deny space, delay a push or force someone to leave cover. An HE can
damage opponents in contested or predictable areas. Grenades can work together
to isolate a fight or force movement into danger.

A lineup is a repeatable throw reference. Knowing its purpose, conditions and
timing matters as well as memorizing the throw. Remaining utility changes later
options. Utility damage or blind time alone does not establish round value.
Exact grenade durations, damage thresholds and current lineups are not specified
by this profile.

## 8. Roles and coordination

An opener seeks first contact or an opening opportunity. An entry takes contested
space with followers ready to exploit contact. Support enables teammates through
utility, trading and other responsibilities. A lurker works away from the group
to control routes, punish movement or activate when team pressure makes it useful.
Going alone does not automatically make a useful lurk.

An anchor defends an area through survival, information, delay and favorable
contact. A rotator supports areas based on changing information. An AWPer shapes
how opponents contest sightlines. An IGL coordinates plans and adaptations.

Roles overlap, especially in PUGs. A role label does not excuse avoidable isolation
or establish that a player had a particular assignment in a specific round.

## 9. T-side play

A default spreads the team to gather information, contest space and retain
options. It does not mean waiting without purpose. An execute combines utility,
routes and timing for a site hit. A split attacks through multiple routes.
A contact play reduces warning until contact or a useful position is reached.

A fake needs a credible threat to draw a response. A regroup reconnects players
for the next action. Scaling means occupying space created by the front players
while keeping the attack connected. A lurk needs timing that interacts with the
main group's action.

Bomb positioning determines whether the objective remains reachable. A bomb left
in isolated enemy-controlled space can force a difficult recovery. Late-round
options shrink when there is insufficient time to travel, clear and plant.

## 10. CT-side play

An anchor balances survival, useful contact, delay and information. Dying early
without a conversion can remove both site resistance and reliable information.
Conceding part of a site can be reasonable when a connected retake is better
than isolated fights.

A rotation trades coverage elsewhere for support here. A lean biases support
toward an area without fully committing. A stack concentrates defenders and
accepts weakness elsewhere. A gamble relies on a prediction when a normal spread
is unattractive or a read is strong enough.

Rotation advice needs an information threshold: what was seen, heard or known,
what remains uncovered, and whether the support can arrive in time. Faster is
not automatically better.

## 11. Numbers, clutches and objectives

With a player advantage, connected positions, trades and time can force opponents
into difficult actions. Becoming completely passive can also surrender useful
control. With a disadvantage, calculated aggression or local concentration may
create a better chance than a routine spread.

A clutch often involves isolating fights, tracking last known locations, using
sound and making the bomb or clock force an opponent's response. An opponent's
actual knowledge may differ from the spectator's view.

After planting, defending the bomb and denying a defuse become central. The plant
position changes useful post-plant positions. An open plant may support distant
defense while making the plant itself riskier. A defuse tap can provoke a peek;
sticking means continuing the defuse. A kit changes the available defuse window.
A retake depends on time, kits, utility, positions and whether regrouping is feasible.

## 12. Economy and weapon literacy

An eco limits investment; a force buy invests despite incomplete funds; a half
buy preserves resources for a later purchase. A full buy aims for a suitably
equipped round. An anti-eco or anti-force is played against a limited enemy buy.
A bonus round can retain carried cheaper weapons rather than immediately upgrade.

Saving preserves equipment for later rounds. Hunting can damage enemy economy
but risks the hunter's equipment. Drops coordinate team resources. Exact buy
decisions depend on team money, carried weapons, loss history and the match rules.

The AK, M4 variants, AWP, Scout, pistols, SMGs and shotguns have different uses,
ranges and commitment costs. An AWP miss can open a recovery window; a rifle can
support sustained or multiple-target fights. A glass cannon means an expensive
weapon with little protective investment; a hero rifle or AWP concentrates
firepower in one player during a limited buy.

Exact prices, damage, fire rates and economy rules need version-specific evidence.
No numerical weapon or economy table is implied by this file.

## 13. Communication and common language

Useful comms convey enemy location, numbers, bomb, damage, utility or intended
actions. Old information and uncertain information need qualification. After
death, concise information helps living teammates retain access to game sounds.

Common terms include:

| Term | Meaning in context |
|---|---|
| Frag / whiff | Kill / miss expected shots. |
| Dink / tagged / lit | Headshot that does not kill / damaged opponent. |
| Wallbang / spam | Shoot through penetrable geometry / fire into a likely position or obstruction. |
| Clear / re-clear | Check an area / check it again after it could become unsafe. |
| Hold / swing / push | Cover an angle / expose to contest it / advance into contested space. |
| Off-angle / one-and-done | Unusual hold / surprise position with limited escape. |
| Trade / refrag | Convert a teammate's death into a kill on the killer. |
| Exec / split / default | Coordinated hit / multiple-route hit / opening structure preserving options. |
| Post-plant / retake | Defend a planted bomb / reclaim the planted site as CT. |
| Save / exits | Preserve equipment / seek kills while opponents leave or survive the round ending. |
| PUG / stack / scrim | Pickup match / group queuing together / organized practice match. |
| Tilt / autopilot | Frustration affecting play / habitual play without updating to the round. |

Words such as "timing," "gap," "bait" and "tap" can mean different things.
Their surrounding situation supplies the meaning.

## 14. Maps and play environment

Callouts identify map-specific areas and can differ by region or team. Familiar
vocabulary includes Mirage mid, connector and palace; Inferno banana and
apartments; Dust2 long, short and tunnels; and Nuke outside, ramp and vent.
Vocabulary alone does not establish exact current geometry or a correct lineup.
This file assigns no favorite map, exhaustive callout knowledge or current map pool.

Solo queue and PUGs can have uncertain roles, communication and utility timing.
A simple coordinated action may be more practical than a detailed five-player
protocol. A stack can establish shared habits; an organized team can practice
defaults and responses. Professional play can involve preparation, conditioning
and support that are invisible in an isolated clip.

Understanding a professional concept does not imply professional execution or
that copying one visible action will reproduce its result.

## 15. Statistics, demos and inference

K/D describes kills relative to deaths; ADR is average damage per round. Opening
duels, trades, survival and utility statistics describe parts of play, not a whole
decision. Composite ratings and trade definitions depend on their producer.

Totals depend on opportunities, role, map, side, opponents and teammates. A
pattern that looks distinctive may also be common among comparable players.
A small number of rounds can support a question without establishing a habit.

A spectator can see enemies the player could not know about. A demo may not
establish voice comms, intent or the reasoning behind a movement. A missing field
does not prove an event did not happen. Sampled movement can omit intermediate
actions; an overhead view cannot answer every first-person mechanics question.

An observed outcome, an explanation and a recommended alternative are separate
claims. Each can require different evidence.

## 16. Learning and useful coaching

Familiarity and usefulness are separate. A reminder of trading can add value by
identifying the specific cover transition where a follower loses sight of the
entry. An unfamiliar label may describe an already familiar idea. A useful
explanation can connect known principles in a new way.

Concrete coaching connects a situation or cue to an available action and explains
the expected benefit and tradeoff. "Rotate faster" leaves out the cue that
justifies rotating. "Use utility" leaves out purpose and timing. A number can
identify a concern without establishing its cause.

Knowing a principle does not prove that someone applied it in a particular
round. Advice about a habit needs actual player evidence. A plausible hypothesis
is different from a demonstrated recurring problem.

Execution can be affected by attention, time pressure and coordination. These
are possible explanations, not assigned traits of this persona. Specificity is
useful when it resolves a decision; extra terminology alone adds little.

## Provenance and limits

`CS2_Experienced_Player_Knowledge_Base.md` and separates game understanding from
its older role-playing and evaluation instructions. Source material includes
coaching/demo-review transcripts. Individual claims here do not yet have a
