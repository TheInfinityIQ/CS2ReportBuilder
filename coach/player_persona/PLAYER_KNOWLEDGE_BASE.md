# CS2 player knowledge base

Version 0.4 — 2026-10-07. Supplied understanding for a simulated player
perspective. No skill level, competitive status, personal history, strengths,
weaknesses, execution consistency or memory gaps are assigned. Knowing these
ideas establishes available understanding, not how well someone plays.

This is an expanded working synthesis. Linked sources identify the particular
creator passages behind applications and qualifications. Tactical advice is
contextual; a creator's recommendation is not an engine rule. Definitions and
connecting explanations are editorial synthesis, not individually verified facts.
An omitted detail is unspecified rather than evidence of ignorance.

The intended finished document is a comprehensive CS2 knowledge compendium:
all distinct, relevant knowledge available in the project's sources, including
coach, professional-player and analyst understanding. This edition is a partial
synthesis toward that goal. Its length and selected examples do not establish
full source coverage. Broad knowledge is intended; personal gameplay ability
and history remain separate.

## Finding and applying the knowledge

This file supplies broad CS2 understanding for interpreting situations and
coaching. It is not a claim of exhaustive recall of every source. The examples
describe hypothetical or source-observed situations, not personal match history.

action changes, and identifies what would change the choice. Being uncertain

situation. Conditional answers can still be concrete: if the replacement player
cannot watch the route, keep coverage or explicitly accept the gap; if coverage
is established, the rotation may become available.

establishes background; it does not initiate a report review. If only excerpts

## 1. Round state and decision quality

A round is a changing combination of information, time, space, equipment and
teammate relationships. The same movement can be strong in one state and poor in
another. Relevant state includes alive players, health, weapons, available
utility, bomb location, remaining time, map control, last known enemies and which
players can actually support each other.

In standard bomb-defusal play, the objective changes after the plant. Before it,
CTs can win by eliminating the Ts or preventing a plant until time expires;
Ts can win by eliminating the CTs or by planting and securing detonation. After
the plant, eliminating every T does not remove the CT requirement to defuse.
A T side with no survivors can still win through detonation. This is why remaining
time and access to the bomb can outweigh remaining players. Other game modes and
custom rules must be identified separately.

Winning the duel does not settle whether taking it was good. An unsupported
fight can succeed because the defender misses; a well-supported entry can die
while enabling a trade and a plant. The decision, execution and outcome are
separate. Similarly, an apparently passive action can preserve a necessary flank
watch, while an aggressive action can generate information or expose teammates.

Counter-Strike rewards converting advantages into round wins. A kill is one
resource, not the objective in isolation. Space, usable information, a forced
rotation, defensive utility spent, a protected plant and saved equipment can all
matter. Whether an action creates value depends on whether somebody can use it.

An account of a decision needs the state before the outcome, not a story invented
afterward. A spectator's certainty about enemy location does not establish what
the player knew.

## 2. Information has content, age and confidence

Seeing an opponent, hearing a sound, seeing a teammate's contact and interpreting
an opponent's utility are different observations. They vary in what they prove.
Two players seen crossing do not identify everyone else. A grenade suggests a
possible thrower or intention; it does not necessarily reveal the whole setup.

Last known position becomes a set of possible positions as time passes. After a
teammate dies or a flash signals pressure, opponents may already have advanced
past the area being watched. Continuing to aim at a smoke edge can miss an enemy
who crossed it earlier. Relevant questions are how much time passed, which routes
were available, and which routes remained watched. [Visualization](#source-visualization)

Reads combine several rounds of behavior with the current state. Repeated utility,
an uncontested route or an opponent who re-peeks can support a prediction.
Changing economy, spawns or a new response can invalidate it. A fixed number of
observations does not guarantee a reliable read.

Available information can also come from teammates' actions when voice comms are
limited. Radar and visible movement can reveal where support is needed or which
route a teammate is committing to. This supports adapting to what teammates are
doing without pretending they will obey a detailed plan. [Radar](#source-radar)

## 3. Communication and uncertainty

A useful call supplies location, count, bomb information, damage, utility or a
planned action while it can still change a teammate's decision. Repeated guesses
can overload attention or induce an unnecessary rotation. Information such as
"one seen" is different from a claim that only one opponent exists.

One source advocates calling only what is confirmed instead of repeatedly
narrating what could happen. That is a recommendation to avoid unsupported calls,
not permission to disguise uncertainty as certainty. An observation and a
hypothesis remain different things. [Information calls](#source-information-calls)

Utility communication depends on shared context. In a PUG, "flashing lane" gives
a recipient more useful information than an unexplained "flashing." A practiced
team may already know the flash, speaker and intended timing, making the shorter
call sufficient. Neither phrasing is universally right. [Flash calls](#source-flash-calls)

After death, information still has value, but prolonged narration can obstruct
living players' attention and sound. Advice to keep dead comms concise differs
from a blanket claim that dead players must never speak.

## 4. Space, control and pressure

Control means an opponent cannot freely use an area because of presence,
information, utility or a credible threat. Standing in an area once does not
guarantee it remains controlled. A player leaving, a smoke fading or a route
becoming unwatched can reopen it.

Space expands routes, planting options, utility positions and ways to pressure
defenders. Its value depends on connectivity: a forward player with no support
may possess territory that the team cannot exploit. Taking control and deciding
what to do with it are separate steps.

Pressure forces attention, utility or movement. A lurk or fake is useful when the
threat affects defenders, not merely because a player is far from teammates.
Allowing pressure to disappear can let opponents concentrate on the main attack.

On Ancient, one reviewed pro example describes a response to lost mid control:
regroup to contest mid or obtain information/space elsewhere, such as a main
route. The transferable lesson is that conceding central control changes the
defensive information problem; the example does not establish only two legal
responses for every round. [Ancient response](#source-ancient-response)

## 5. Timing and committing to a plan

Timing is a window created by distance, spawn, speed, sound, utility and enemy
attention. It can mean arriving early, acting while someone is occupied,
coordinating two actions, or the accidental overlap of movements. "Bad timing"
without identifying the window does not explain a decision.

A plan needs a follow-up when its initial advantage disappears. One reviewed
coaching passage distinguishes continuing while pressure creates a favorable
chance from forcing through a choke after the surprise is gone. Falling back and
reconsidering is an option, not proof of passivity. [Plan B](#source-plan-b)

The clock limits which alternatives are physically possible. Waiting out utility
can improve a fight but remove the time needed to travel, clear and plant.
Redirecting can avoid stacked defenders but create an impossible journey for the
bomb. No universal "execute at this many seconds" rule follows without the route,
equipment and current positions.

Committing is not the same as refusing to update. It means carrying out an
available action with appropriate coordination while responding to new evidence.

## 6. Crosshair placement and clearing order

Crosshair placement anticipates the location and movement of a plausible target.
Head height changes with elevation and posture; peek width changes where a
defender should expect the target to appear. Holding directly against the corner
can require a large correction if the opponent swings wider.

Pre-aiming a common position can help, but committing to it too early may skip a
closer off-angle. A reviewed demonstration first clears the intervening geometry,
then prepares for the common position. The underlying idea is an exposure-aware
clearing order, not mechanically following every corner with the crosshair.
[Clearing](#source-clearing)

Movement can bring a prepared crosshair onto an angle. Mouse correction still
matters when the opponent appears somewhere else. Strong setup reduces the
required adjustment; it does not guarantee that no adjustment is needed.

A flick, tracking correction and pre-aim solve different problems. An unusually
large flick can indicate poor preparation, unexpected information or a legitimate
surprise. One miss does not isolate its cause.

## 7. Movement, stopping and exposure

Movement changes exposure, information and weapon accuracy. Counter-strafing is a
method of stopping lateral movement quickly. The relevant firing question is
whether the weapon was sufficiently accurate at that moment, not whether the
player used a particular word for stopping.

Pathing determines how many positions become threats at once. A route that skips
unchecked space can produce a duel the player never prepared for. A route that
clears everything slowly can also lose a trade window or expose teammates.

Crouching changes the player's target shape and movement options. A reviewed
peeking lesson recommends using deliberate crouch peeks where a fight can be
isolated, rather than becoming stationary while exposed to several angles.
A recovered short independently makes the same distinction between purposeful
crouching and an automatic crouch before every shot. These are situational
recommendations, not a claim that crouching is always wrong.
[Peeking](#source-peeking), [Crouch choice](#source-crouch-choice)

"Ferrari peek" describes the appearance of a fast swing, not a separate engine
ability. A recovered demonstration connects approach geometry, crosshair setup
and stopping to a particular fast peek. Its close-to-wall approach is not a
general rule that proximity to cover always grants better vision.
[Fast peek](#source-fast-peek)

## 8. Holding, jiggling and weapon-specific exceptions

One movement lesson recommends short, quiet lateral movement while rifle holding,
with stopping for the shot and moving again afterward. The stated goal is to
avoid being an easy static target and to be ready for the next fight.
[Active holding](#source-active-holding)

Another passage explicitly distinguishes a posted AWP or Deagle hold: that player
may wait still to preserve the intended accurate shot. Searching for information
with a jiggle is different from already holding an expected duel.
[Posted hold](#source-posted-hold)

These passages should not be collapsed into either "always move" or "never move."
Weapon accuracy, the purpose of exposure, opponent timing and the available cover
change the decision. A movement cue that creates footsteps can also reveal a
position that was supposed to remain unknown.

An off-angle can avoid common pre-aim and expose the attacker to another threat.
Its surprise can be consumed after contact. Escape and support still matter.
Source titles promising free kills are rhetorical; a position never guarantees a
kill simply because it is unusual.

## 9. Taps, bursts, sprays and resets

Shot selection depends on range, exposed target area, weapon, recovery and the
number of opponents who may engage. A spray can commit to a close duel; a short
burst can suit a small exposed target; controlled single shots can suit another
range or timing.

One creator strongly recommends prioritizing sprays to avoid unsuccessful
one-tap attempts. In the surrounding passage, the creator explicitly retains
exceptions for long distances and headshot angles. Another reviewed coaching
passage prefers more tapping/bursting at long range and sprays at closer ranges.
These are compatible contextual recommendations, not a universal spray percentage
or a requirement to imitate a professional player's style.
[Spray recommendation](#source-spray-recommendation), [Range](#source-range)

Recoil control, spread and recovery are distinct. Moving a crosshair onto the
next target does not reset the weapon's firing state. A spray transfer accounts
for the existing spray; a fresh burst requires its own timing. Exact bullet
patterns and recovery times require weapon-specific evidence.

Mechanical explanations need the view and sampling necessary to see them. An
overhead position trail cannot establish exact crosshair placement, recoil input
or the timing of a firing error.

## 10. AWP understanding

The AWP can deny a sightline, enable a teammate to cross, create an opening or
pressure a rotation. Supporting a route is different from passively waiting for
a kill that will not affect the team's objective.

After contact, opponents have information about the AWP. Repositioning can make
the next engagement harder to anticipate, but leaving a required line unwatched
also has a cost. A reviewed Inferno lesson encourages varying starting positions
instead of using the same B position every round. [AWP roles](#source-awp-roles)

Inferno short is presented by two reviewed lessons as useful for maneuvering,
fallbacks and flexible sightlines. Their strong frequency recommendations are
coaching preferences within those setups, not a prescribed percentage for every
AWPer. Team coverage and opponent utility determine whether the option works.
[Inferno roles](#source-inferno-roles)

Equipment decisions extend beyond the current buy. A reviewed AWPer preserves
cash while teammates force so the later AWP purchase remains available. This is
an example of planning an equipment role across rounds, not a fixed instruction
to buy a Deagle or save a particular amount every time. [AWP money](#source-awp-money)

## 11. Training, warm-up and transfer

Practice can isolate a mechanic; a warm-up prepares for playing now. A reviewed
training passage distinguishes intensive mechanical work from a shorter warm-up
and warns that doing too much before playing can be counterproductive. It does
not establish a universal fatigue threshold or medical exercise prescription.
[Practice](#source-practice)

Different exercises supply different contexts. Bots can make movement and small
corrections repeatable. Deathmatch creates frequent fights but does not reproduce
all information, utility or team decisions. Retake situations add different
objective and sound demands. Improvement in an exercise does not by itself prove
that the behavior transfers to matches.

One coach recommends removing deathmatch audio for an exercise focused on visual
crosshair work and reaction. The explanation is about the exercise's purpose,
not a claim that sound is bad during matches or that all deathmatch must be
played this way. [Deathmatch purpose](#source-deathmatch-purpose)

Another source recommends reviewing with a single focus, such as pathing. A
focused session can expose a repeatable pattern without requiring simultaneous
attention to every possible issue. [Study focus](#source-study-focus)

## 12. Settings and preferences

Sensitivity, DPI/eDPI, resolution, aspect ratio, crosshair, viewmodel, frame rate,
audio and radar affect the interface through which a player performs actions.
They do not identify a player's intelligence, discipline or rank.

An eDPI value combines mouse DPI and in-game sensitivity; it is not a complete
description of comfort or performance. A quoted recommended range is not proof
that every value outside it is unusable. Changing a setting can have adaptation
costs without making every change harmful.

The reviewed resolution source expresses a personal dislike of native resolution
while acknowledging player differences and its visual-awareness advantages.
Resolution preference therefore stays a preference, rather than a rule that one
display format is uncompetitive.
[Resolution](#source-resolution)

A visible, usable crosshair helps with a task, but its appearance does not prove
that a player's mechanics are correct. Exact performance claims about hardware
or latency need measurements beyond a creator's assertion.

## 13. Utility purpose and conversion

A grenade uses time, inventory and attention while the weapon may be unavailable.
Its purpose can be damage, delay, space, a crossing, a favorable fight, information
denial or a credible fake. A throw that appears to do little damage can still
support the objective; a spectacular blind or damage number may be unused.

A reviewed utility lesson connects a flash to a recipient who can actually peek
and connects fire to somebody positioned to punish a displaced defender. A
grenade can otherwise spend a resource while giving the opponent time to return.
[Utility purpose](#source-utility-purpose)

The same lesson's skeptical treatment of distraction is not a ban on fakes. A
separate reviewed conditioning example uses utility to shape expectations even
without an immediate fight. The distinction is between a credible intended
effect and a throw with no usable follow-up. [Conditioning](#source-conditioning)

A reviewed Cache analysis notes the moment when a molly makes a defender leave
cover and exposes a kill opportunity. The transferable cue is displacement and
readiness to catch it, not an exact watch duration for every grenade.
[Displacement](#source-displacement)

A reviewed multi-grenade sequence requires teammate drops and a ready attack.
Shared stock can let one thrower perform several tasks while others retain weapons
for contact. The timing, safe pickup area and team inventory determine whether
that division of labor works. [Shared utility](#source-shared-utility)

## 14. Smokes, re-smokes and visibility

Smokes reshape sightlines. They can isolate a defender, protect a crossing,
prevent a support player from contributing, delay entry or conceal repositioning.
They also remove information from the thrower's side. An isolated fight is only
an advantage if the relevant teammate can take it.

Placement matters beyond whether the smoke nominally landed. Depth can change
where opponents stand, whether they can spam a likely plant and which side has
room to move. A reviewed Ancient donut example discusses a deeper placement as
part of a plan to plant and later take that space. The transcript's claim of
safety does not prove that every spam route is eliminated.
[Donut smoke](#source-donut-smoke)

Re-smoking preserves a temporary relationship long enough for a teammate to
scale or finish another action. It can also expend the smoke needed later.
A reviewed Nuke lurk explicitly holds a smoke for a post-plant door, ramp or
reaction instead of using it with no end goal. [Retained smoke](#source-retained-smoke)

Valve documented reducing CT incendiary duration and spread in May 2024. This is
a dated example of why older smoke/fire interactions and timings cannot simply
be treated as current constants. [Valve update](#source-valve-update)

## 15. Flashes and recipient readiness

A useful flash has an intended opponent, recipient and action window. Its landing
time relative to the recipient's exposure matters. A blind that ends before a
teammate can act may produce no fight advantage.

Supporting a teammate already fighting can require a flash that detonates behind
their view, rather than making them turn away and lose the duel. That is the
geometry of a reviewed example; actual facing, surfaces and other teammates can
change who is blinded. [Support flash](#source-support-flash)

Anti-flash positioning anticipates a flash while retaining a plan for the next
contact. Self-flashes and support flashes have different constraints because the
thrower may need to recover their weapon before acting.

Repeated flashes can create another window, but they do not guarantee that the
second blinds an opponent. Throw timing, warning, cover and opponent behavior
remain relevant. A lineup described only as "aim here" is not a complete throw
instruction without its visual reference.

## 16. Fire, HE and sequencing

Fire can delay, clear, divide or force movement. The desired effect determines
whether stopping opponents is helpful. In a reviewed Mirage setup, slowing
attackers with fire would interfere with the intended prepared fight and allow
them to reconsider their route. The recommendation favors damage without that
delay in that state, not a universal preference for HE over a molly.
[Delay exception](#source-delay-exception)

A recovered Inferno short discusses an early grenade, then fire, then a smoke
and a subsequent peek. It contains useful sequencing, but the throw descriptions
depend on the video image and the opponent's arrival window. It is a tactical
option with prerequisites, not an automatic script for every spawn.
[Banana sequence](#source-banana-sequence)

Keeping an HE can create a late-round option against a known position. The
reviewed source also acknowledges that early grenades have more predictable
targets and that a late grenade may miss. The comparison is expected value now
versus a possible future use, not "always keep the HE."
[HE timing](#source-he-timing)

Exact damage thresholds, grenade spread, sound feedback, penetration and whether
a particular throw extinguishes or reveals something require appropriate game
version evidence. They are not established merely by matching a transcript quote.

## 17. Trades, spacing and connected routes

Trading prevents an opponent from taking an uncontested kill and resetting.
Tradeability depends on reachable sightlines, timing, weapon readiness and the
defender's escape, not a universal distance in meters.

The entry also has responsibility for the gap to the follower. A reviewed team
demo praises spacing even though the follower fails to convert the fight:
appropriate setup and successful execution are different. [Spacing](#source-spacing)

Being directly stacked can obstruct a teammate's retreat. A reviewed pro example
explains why giving the front player room to move is part of supporting a trade.
Spacing therefore balances closeness, visibility, movement and the risk that one
spray or utility disables both. [Retreat room](#source-retreat-room)

A reviewed Mirage entry example recommends the first two players use connected
pathing: a follower moving to Tetris cannot necessarily trade a teammate killed
by an under-balcony defender. Its wording calls this a rule of thumb. Identical
paths are not a guarantee of a trade and do not replace checking the actual
firing line. [Entry routes](#source-entry-routes)

Following contact with the bomb or utility in hand may miss a fleeting trade
opportunity. A reviewed Ancient donut situation makes that specific error
visible. It does not require chasing every death through unsafe space.
[Donut trade](#source-donut-trade)

## 18. Roles and team function

An entry creates access to contested space; an opener seeks initial contact.
The second player converts attention or damage into a fight. Support includes
utility, coverage, bomb responsibility and enabling another player's action.
The IGL coordinates plans, pace and adaptation. A lurker supplies a threat or
information away from the main group. An anchor retains area responsibility;
a rotator can support more than one area.

These functions overlap. Labels do not settle who should act in a particular
round. A support player may need to take the available opening; an entry should
not run far ahead simply to satisfy the label. A star's visible freedom can be
enabled by other players' coverage rather than an independent license to roam.

The same kill count can represent different opportunities. Holding a flank,
forcing utility or supplying a flash can enable a round without a high score.
Conversely, a nominal support action can contribute little if nobody can use it.

Role knowledge is available to this persona; no preferred role or habitual
behavior is assigned to it.

## 19. Defaults, executes, contact plays and changes of pace

A default develops information, space and options before a finish. It is neither
necessarily slow nor synonymous with spreading every player alone. A reviewed
Dust2 coaching passage develops a tradeable lower-tunnels/short opening before
considering the B split, rather than assuming the finish will work from spawn.
[Default development](#source-default-development)

An execute links utility, routes and timing. A split creates threats from more
than one approach, but separated groups can still arrive in unrelated fights.
A contact approach preserves warning time by limiting noise/utility until contact
or useful access, at the cost of fewer early protections.

A reviewed pop-play example gets attackers through the entrance before their
smokes land, exploiting the moment defenders prepare counter-utility. This is a
timing concept, not a guarantee that running before the smokes is safe. It needs
coordinated recipients, routes and a plausible defender response.
[Pop timing](#source-pop-timing)

Changing pace can punish an expectation, but pacing is subordinate to the current
information and available routes. A failed first plan need not become five
players repeatedly pushing the same blocked choke.

## 20. Lurking and activating

A lurk can hold a rotation, punish aggression, deny a route or create a second
threat as the main attack occupies defenders. Waiting is useful when it preserves
that function; waiting with no interaction can leave the main group unsupported.

One Mirage coaching passage explains that a palace/apps lurk becomes harder when
teammates cannot generate mid pressure. Joining the group to establish that
pressure may create better later lurks. "Lurker" is not a reason to ignore the
team's current problem. [Lurk pressure](#source-lurk-pressure)

An opening kill can consume the lurker's safe timing. A reviewed passage advises
leaving after obtaining the advantage because several awkward lines can converge
on the position. The cue is changing exposure and support, not an instruction to
retreat after every kill. [Lurk exit](#source-lurk-exit)

In a reviewed Inferno round, a player without utility needs to reconnect when the
bomb commits banana. Supporting a feasible objective can matter more than a
disconnected ideal alternative. That does not establish that an unsafe bomb
carrier should always be followed blindly. [Reconnect](#source-reconnect)

## 21. Anchoring, rotations and defensive coverage

Anchoring combines survival, delay, information and fight selection. A useful
death can enable a trade or buy critical time; dying without either may collapse
coverage. A kill is not the sole measure of successful defense.

A rotation exchanges current coverage for prospective support. A reviewed anchor
rotation is criticized because enemy commitment is unconfirmed and the supposed
replacement is not actually covering the site. The lesson is coverage handover
and evidence, not "the anchor always rotates last."
[Rotation handover](#source-rotation-handover)

A lean moves help closer without a full commitment. A stack concentrates power
while knowingly leaving another route weaker. Information, economy and fallback
options determine whether that gamble makes sense. Remaining in an even spread
can also be poor when the round demands a concentrated chance.

On Inferno, a reviewed example leaves a banana player with information and
recycled utility while shifting another defender toward A. The same passage
shows the vulnerability when a B rush arrives and help is poorly positioned.
A strengthened A setup does not establish a free improvement overall.
[Cheating a defender](#source-cheating-defender)

## 22. Numbers and clutches

A numerical lead matters when players can create connected fights and force the
opponent to act. Consecutive isolated duels can discard that resource. Excessive
passivity can also hand over access, information or a plant.

A disadvantage may require a calculated risk: an isolated opening, local numbers
or an information play. The risk must improve a plausible winning path rather
than merely express frustration. Not all routes can be covered with fewer players.

A reviewed pro clutch discussion explains accepting an uncovered flank when
covering everything would eliminate the available chance. This is a conscious
tradeoff under poor odds, not evidence that flanks are irrelevant.
[Clutch gamble](#source-clutch-gamble)

Another reviewed example uses opponents' incomplete knowledge of a dropped bomb
and apparent B pressure to induce a fight. The mechanism is manipulating the
opponent's objective inference. It depends on what they observed and where the
bomb can be recovered; the action is not a reusable script without that state.
[Bomb uncertainty](#source-bomb-uncertainty)

## 23. Plants, post-plants and retakes

Plant choice changes which positions can defend the bomb and which defuse routes
are dangerous. A plant safe during its animation may be harder to defend later;
an exposed plant may make sense when teammates actually control the threats.

A reviewed Mirage post-plant weighs CT, jungle, ramp and flank responsibilities
before taking new space. Its warning against a particular jungle plan does not
establish that jungle is always wrong. The useful question is whether the
survivors can cover the objective and each other after repositioning.
[Plant relationships](#source-plant-relationships)

A recovered short emphasizes making retakers clear protected positions rather
than voluntarily leaving a strong connected setup to seek a kill. It does not
prove a crossfire is unbreakable, despite the creator's emphatic phrasing.
[Post-plant restraint](#source-post-plant-restraint)

A tap can make an opponent react; sticking continues the defuse. The opponent
may ignore a tap, use utility or infer a fake. Kits, time, distance and remaining
utility alter the available response. No sound-masking trick is established here
as a guaranteed defuse method.

A retake needs a plan to reach the bomb, manage the relevant defenders and finish
in time. Grouping can enable a trade; splitting can apply useful pressure.
Neither is automatically correct without the current routes and timing.

## 24. Economy, survival and hunting

An eco preserves resources; a force invests despite incomplete funds; a half buy
balances this round with a later purchase; a full buy equips the intended round.
A bonus retains carried weapons when upgrading may not justify its cost. A drop
redistributes equipment within the team.

The useful economic unit is the team across several rounds, including carried
equipment and role requirements. A won round with casualties can leave a fragile
following buy. Losing a round with useful saved equipment can preserve a better
future chance than losing everything.

A reviewed Dust2 round ends with only one survivor despite the win, requiring
replacement purchases and drops instead of building reserves. A separate Inferno
team review tracks costly wins before an eventual weak buy. A winning streak
therefore does not establish a healthy bank. Estimate the opponent's equipment
from recent survival, saved guns and spending opportunities, with uncertainty
about unseen cash and purchases. [Costly win](#source-costly-win),
[Economic tracking](#source-economic-tracking)

Buying is a coordinated allocation, not five independent shopping decisions.
Check who can equip whom, which weapons are already carried and whether the plan
needs particular utility. A reviewed player buys a Deagle while the team has
rifles and commits elsewhere; the criticism connects equipment and participation
to the team's round. It does not imply that every individual must buy identically:
the planned future AWP purchase in section 10 is a supported exception.
[Team buy](#source-team-buy), [AWP money](#source-awp-money)

Against a limited buy, protecting dropped rifles can matter as much as collecting
the next kill. A reviewed Mirage example covers a dropped AK with smoke before
swapping weapons, then anticipates an opponent searching for it. This is an
equipment-denial mechanism, with an inventory and attention cost; an opponent's
eco is not proof that all their weapons are harmless.
[Equipment denial](#source-equipment-denial)

Saving compares remaining winning opportunities with equipment value and the
next round. A reviewed coaching passage re-evaluates the round after a kill and
recognizes that the available route still does not create a realistic win.
The fact that a kill occurred does not force continuing the attempt.
[Save decision](#source-save-decision)

The marginal value of saving depends on what it changes. A reviewed pro round
considers an attempt more understandable because two saved rifles would still
leave a poor next buy. The same narrator cautions against copying that attempt

## 27. Statistics and the meaning of a finding

K/D compares kills and deaths; ADR summarizes damage per round. Opening attempts,
opening success, trades, survival, utility damage and blind time capture different
parts of participation. Composite metrics depend on their producer's definitions.

Opportunity matters. A player cannot get a conventional trade if nobody engages
within a reachable line. A position with frequent contact can raise some totals
while exposing more deaths. Role, side, map, opponents and teammate behavior
change the comparison group.

A reviewed anti-eco analysis distinguishes damage against limited buys from
damage against full buys, and uses opening participation to discuss whether a
player changes their style. The transferable lesson is stratifying the context,
not copying historical player values as current baselines. [Buy context](#source-buy-context)

An aggregate association does not establish causation. More wins when holding
than when entering a holder may also be true for other players. A personal
recommendation needs evidence of a relevant individual pattern and available
alternatives, not a familiar general disadvantage presented as unique.

Small samples, missing observations and overlapping categories can make a precise
number misleading. A useful statistic can identify where to investigate without
settling the reason for the pattern.

## 28. Demo evidence and reconstruction limits

The demo's observable state differs from a player's knowledge. Enemy locations
visible to a spectator are not automatically player information. A sound that
exists in the recording does not settle whether it was audible through all other
sounds or attended to in the moment.

Comms, intent, assignment and reasoning may be unavailable. Position samples can
miss intermediate movements; an overhead view cannot replace the first-person
evidence required for recoil, exact aim or a subtle peek. A missing data column
does not prove an event did not occur.

A useful explanation separates the observed action from the proposed mechanism
and from a better alternative. The alternative must have been available with the
time, equipment and information present then.

Death review can be a productive focus. One reviewed creator concentrates on
why duels fail in personal PUG review. Another assigns one topic such as pathing
for a study session. These are review methods with different scopes; neither
establishes that only deaths matter or that all other play is irrelevant.
[Death review](#source-death-review), [Study focus](#source-study-focus)

## 29. Mental state, learning and execution

Knowledge, attention and execution are different. A player can understand a
principle without the available evidence showing whether it was applied.
Conversely, a failed outcome is not proof of ignorance, tilt or lack of confidence.

A reviewed discussion separates feeling frustration from allowing it to dictate
the next action. It describes constructive communication as an alternative to
flaming. It does not guarantee an emotional reset or establish a trait of this
persona. [Mental response](#source-mental-response)

Repeated revenge peeks, distracted preparation or changes in attention may be
possible explanations when supported by observed behavior. They should not be
invented simply to explain an unfavorable statistic.

Learning can connect a familiar idea to a more useful cue. Knowing trading is
different from noticing the particular cover transition where tradeability
disappears. Knowing utility purpose is different from having a clear plan for
who catches a displaced defender.

## 30. Familiarity, novelty and actionable understanding

A concept can be familiar yet useful in a new situation. A reminder can be
valuable when it reveals where an understood principle applies. A novel label
can add little if it only renames something already understood.

Specific coaching connects a cue, available action, mechanism and tradeoff.
"Rotate faster" omits the information threshold. "Improve spacing" omits the
line or timing that stopped the trade. "Be confident" omits the decision or
execution to change. Conversely, adding many terms or statistics does not ensure
a usable instruction.

The presence of a plausible tactical explanation does not prove the report's
claim about this player. Factual correctness, personal relevance, comprehensibility
and practical usefulness can differ. A report may explain an important general
idea without demonstrating that it is the player's recurring problem.

## 31. Confidence, disagreements and version boundaries

Fundamental language and qualitative decision relationships differ from numerical
mechanics, visual lineups, historical team protocols and current meta claims.
The latter need their date, map build, rule set or visual evidence preserved.

Sources can use emphatic language for teaching. "Always," "free," "unbreakable"
and "everyone" do not remove the described conditions. The reviewed material
contains useful tensions: active holding versus posted accuracy, spending utility
early versus reserving it, delaying attackers versus drawing them into a setup,
and grouping versus preserving a separate pressure route.

These are often different problems rather than contradictory facts. When the
underlying conditions are unresolved, the disagreement remains unresolved. The

## 33. Equipment readiness and information costs

Weapon choice changes the fights a team can take. Compare the intended distance,
target exposure, repeat-shot needs, ability to move between cover, ammunition,
purchase cost and supporting utility. Weapon names alone do not settle a duel.
An expensive gun on an isolated, exposed route can be less usable than equipment
suited to the planned fight. The AWP's sightline control differs from a rifle's
ability to handle several contacts; its user still needs a plan for missed shots,
close pressure and routes left uncovered.

Armor and helmets change vulnerability; kits change available defuse time.
Their purchase value depends on expected opponents, carried equipment, team
coverage and the round's purpose. Damage depends on weapon, hit location,
distance, armor and intervening material. A remembered damage threshold from
another weapon or build is insufficient to certify a kill. Low health can alter
who takes first contact or uses utility, without turning one player into an
automatically expendable scout.

Readiness includes what is in the player's hands. Drawing a grenade, collecting
the bomb, reloading or changing weapons can remove the immediate response to
contact. A useful reload window combines cover, expected arrival time, teammate
coverage and enough ammunition for the next task. Reloading immediately after
every kill may surrender an available trade or pressure window; never reloading
can leave an otherwise favorable fight without ammunition. The magazine and
sound changes in section 36 add further costs to that choice.

Pre-firing and smoke spam can punish an expected position or discourage a cross.
They also spend ammunition and disclose activity; a hit does not establish that
every bullet was well chosen. Wallbang value depends on the actual material,
weapon and likely target. When comparing an available shot with a reload or
reposition, consider what the opponent can do during the resulting downtime.

Sound changes information and pace. Running for a necessary trade can be worth
revealing presence; walking to preserve a surprise can miss the fight entirely.
The question is who could hear an action in that state and what they could infer,
not whether walking or running is universally superior. A reviewed Ancient
execute uses a smoke intended to counter expected fire; without that fire, the
smoke's activation could warn defenders about the attack. Utility can conceal
one route while revealing intent. [Sound and intent](#source-sound-and-intent)

An absent sound is limited evidence. The enemy might be outside audible range,
using a quieter action, occupied by another sound or following another route.
Sniper un-scoping and silent reload specifically changed in September 2026;
an old audio rule must not determine a new demo's interpretation. A recording
also does not establish everything a player heard or noticed.

## 34. Shared language and distinct concepts

These are editorial definitions of common usage. Local callouts and metric
providers can use different meanings; the associated mechanism matters more
than forcing one vocabulary onto every team.

| Term | Meaning and distinction |
|---|---|
| Pick / opening | An initial elimination or sought duel. It need not create safe entry to a site. |
| Entry / second | First access to contested space and the player positioned to convert its contact. These are round functions, not fixed ability labels. |
| Trade / refrag | Responding to a teammate's engagement or death by engaging the opponent. Close distance alone does not create the necessary firing line. |
| Bait | Drawing an opponent's attention or action. It can describe deliberate teamwork or harmful abandonment; identify what was enabled. |
| Bait-and-switch | One player's contact draws attention toward a second threat or protected position. Its timing and coverage matter. |
| Crossfire | Two connected threats that complicate the opponent's clearing. Two players watching the same corridor are not automatically an effective crossfire. |
| Swing / wide swing | Exposing farther past cover to seek a fight or displace the opponent's aim. Width trades surprise and movement against extra exposure. |
| Jiggle / shoulder / jump spot | Brief exposure intended to obtain information or provoke a response. These techniques differ in visibility, movement and risk. |
| Pre-aim / prefire | Preparing the crosshair for an expected position / firing based on that expectation before confirming the target. |
| Off-angle / headshot angle | A less expected hold / a position exposing a small target, often primarily the head. They are different properties. |
| Anti-flash / pop flash | Preparing to avoid a flash / a flash designed to leave little warning before detonation. Neither assures the following fight. |
| Scaling | Advancing with controlled coverage and usable support. It need not mean always walking or always rushing. |
| Choke / lane / choke isolation | A constrained route / a line of access / temporarily removing a threat to simplify the remaining fight. |
| Default / set execute | A distributed opening that seeks information and control / a coordinated attack with assigned tasks. A default can develop into an execute. |
| Contact / rush / pop | An approach acting on contact / an early committed attack / a coordinated emergence. Teams may use these labels differently. |
| Fake / conditioning | Presenting a threat to redirect attention / building expectations through repeated behavior. Opponent reactions determine value. |
| Lurk / late lurk | A separate threat or information route / activating it as the main action develops. Being far away alone is insufficient. |
| Anchor / lean / rotate / stack | Maintain area responsibility / move help closer / transfer coverage / concentrate defenders while accepting weakness elsewhere. |
| Retake / post-plant | Re-entering a lost site to secure the objective / the state after planting, with defense now organized around the bomb. |
| Tap / stick / fake defuse | Start a defuse briefly / continue it / use its cue to provoke a response. Knowledge of time and kits changes the choices. |
| Eco / force / half buy / bonus | Preserve funds / invest with incomplete resources / balance current and next purchase / retain carried weapons into the next round. |
| Anti-eco / exits / hunt | Play against a limited buy / late kills around escape / actively seek surviving equipment. None makes weapon sacrifice free. |
| Dry peek / re-peek | Expose without enabling utility / expose again after contact. Repeating a duel changes the opponent's information. |
| Gap / timing window | A temporarily uncovered route / a period when a particular action can work. Neither is a permanent property of a position. |
| Reset | Can mean aim/recoil recovery, disengaging to rethink, or weakened buying resources after losses. Specify which. |
| ADR / K/D / KAST | Average damage per round / kills relative to deaths / a provider-defined measure involving kills, assists, survival or trades. Check eligibility and trade definitions. |

## 35. Worked situations and changing conditions

These constructed situations combine the preceding concepts. They illustrate
reasoning, not new historical evidence or personal experiences. The alternatives
are conditional on the stated information; different observations can reverse
the preferred action.

### A. Close enough to follow, unable to trade

On Mirage, the lead attacker moves under balcony while the follower remains
around Tetris. The lead dies to a defender the follower cannot see. Closing
distance alone cannot fix the blocked line. Before contact, choose a connected
route or assign utility that removes that defender; at contact, a prepared
weapon and actual line determine whether a trade exists. If an unexpected second
defender covers the connecting route, blindly chasing the death creates another
isolated fight. This follows the reviewed entry-route example in section 17.
[Entry routes](#source-entry-routes)

### B. Useful flash, unusable timing

A support player blinds the intended defender, but the entry is still clearing
an intervening corner. The utility's effect occurs before its recipient is ready.
Improve the timing by connecting the flash to the entry's exposure cue or choose
a throw that supports the intervening fight. If the entry is already fighting,
consider whether the flash blinds that teammate or forces them to turn. Enemy
blind time alone cannot settle whether the flash helped.
[Utility purpose](#source-utility-purpose), [Support flash](#source-support-flash)

### C. A promising rotation with no handover

Pressure is reported elsewhere and the anchor starts leaving. The supposed
replacement is behind smoke and cannot watch the route. Re-establish coverage,
move help closer without fully leaving, or explicitly accept a weakened site
when the evidence warrants concentrating elsewhere. Confirmed bomb commitment
and a usable replacement change the choice. Keeping the anchor in place despite
those changes can waste a chance to support the actual objective.
[Rotation handover](#source-rotation-handover)

### D. The same molly creates opposite results

Fire that stops attackers at a choke can buy time for an arriving rotator.
In a prepared crossfire intended to catch their advance, the same delay can
let them reconsider and bypass the fight. Identify whether the intended value
is time, displacement or entry into the setup before recommending the grenade.
Its damage total alone does not describe that value.
[Delay exception](#source-delay-exception)

### E. A kill does not settle the save decision

A CT gets a kill during a late retake. Re-evaluate routes, remaining opponents,
defuse access and available time. If the kill removes the last blocker and the
objective is reachable, continuing may now be possible. If the route remains
too long, the kill has not repaired the timing. Then compare preserved equipment
with the next buy. A poor next buy can raise the value of a realistic gamble;
it cannot supply missing time. [Save decision](#source-save-decision),
[Marginal save value](#source-marginal-save-value)

### F. Space gained, objective disconnected

An attacker gains mid while opponents pressure another route. The gained space
is useful only if the team can exploit it without stranding the bomb. Identify
who watches the carrier's path, whether the new route threatens defenders and
when support can reconnect. Remaining distant may create valuable pressure or
may leave a required plant impossible; location alone cannot distinguish them.
[Anubis space](#source-anubis-space)

### G. Low ADR, uncertain cause

A report attributes low ADR to poor aim. First separate limited and full buys,
roles, contact frequency and mechanically observable evidence. Low damage can
come from few usable engagements, missed shots, poor routes or other causes.
A first-person clip supporting a repeatable error improves the explanation;
the aggregate number does not isolate it. More context should refine the claim,
not add an invented confidence or personality problem.
[Buy context](#source-buy-context)

### H. Good lesson, unproven personal relevance

"Trade your entry" names a familiar concept. A report showing where the follower
lost the firing line, which alternative route was available and what threat that
route exposes can add usable understanding. If the clip already shows a blocked
route and absent support, merely demanding faster movement is misleading.
The idea can be understood even when evidence of a recurring personal problem
is missing. Familiarity, accuracy and usefulness remain separate judgments.
